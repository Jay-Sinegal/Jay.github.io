I Don't Just Plan Events. I Build the System Behind Them.

What building events taught me about brand strategy, operations, sponsorship, marketing, music licensing, copyright, and creating experiences that actually matter.

By Jaylen Sinegal · September 7, 2026

THIS SHIT IS HARDER THAN IT LOOKS.

Planning an event is easy to underestimate.

People see the finished product.

They see the athletes. The lights. The music. The sponsors. The cameras. The crowd. The uniforms. The photographs. The social media posts.

They see the moment.

I learned to see everything that has to happen BEFORE the moment exists.

Registration. Scheduling. Contracts. Insurance. Waivers. Equipment. Staffing. Communication. Marketing. Sponsorship. Media. Customer experience. Safety. Revenue. Contingency planning.

Every piece has to work.

And when one piece doesn't work, somebody eventually feels it.

That's what building events taught me.

An event isn't simply something you schedule. It's an experience you engineer.

My event planning and artist coordination experience spans multiple organizations and communities. As an independent professional, I've collaborated with FlightTime Athletics on program development and event strategy, worked with Acadiana Biddy Basketball on athletic development and competition strategy, and served as an event planner and artist coordinator for Allure For Moore. That work gave me firsthand experience connecting athletic development, business strategy, marketing, sponsorship, operations, media, and community engagement into one integrated system.

That experience changed how I think about brands.

It also changed how I think about business.

I stopped looking at an event as an isolated production.

I started looking at the entire machine behind it.

I BUILT MORE THAN AN EVENT. I BUILT AN OPERATING SYSTEM.

The first major lesson was simple:

The event is only one part of the business.

A strong event sits inside a larger architecture.

For me, that architecture looked more like:

Audience → Offer → Registration → Operations → Experience → Sponsorship → Media → Content → Follow-Up → Revenue

Every stage affects the next.

You can't build the experience without understanding the audience.

You can't sell the experience without defining the offer.

You can't market the offer without having the operational capacity to deliver it.

You can't create valuable sponsorship opportunities without understanding your audience.

And you can't maximize the event if you let everything end when the lights turn off.

That's when event planning became business strategy for me.

I wasn't simply asking:

"How do we make this event happen?"

I was asking:

"How does this event strengthen the business?"

That's a much bigger question.

A well-organized event communicates something.

Your operations are speaking for your brand whether you realize it or not.

That realization became incredibly important to the way I approach brand strategy.

Branding isn't simply logos, colors, fonts, photography, or social media.

Branding is the expectation you create and the experience you deliver.

IF MUSIC IS PART OF THE EXPERIENCE, RIGHTS MANAGEMENT IS PART OF THE BUSINESS.

This is where my Music Business background matters.

Music isn't just atmosphere.

Music is intellectual property.

When a company uses copyrighted music at an event, inside a business, in promotional content, on a website, through an app, in advertising, or as part of another commercial experience, the business needs to understand what rights are actually being used.

That's not something I believe belongs at the bottom of an event checklist.

It belongs in the planning process.

The U.S. Copyright Office explains that public-performance licensing for musical works is commonly handled through performing rights organizations, or PROs, including ASCAP, BMI, and SESAC. PRO licenses can provide public-performance rights for works within their respective repertoires.

But here's where businesses need to pay attention:

A PRO license is not a universal license to use every piece of music in every way.

Different uses can involve different rights.

  • Public performance
  • Synchronization
  • Mechanical reproduction
  • Master use
  • Digital performance

Other permissions may apply depending on the use.

BMI specifically states that it licenses performing rights but does not license mechanical or synchronization rights.

That distinction matters.

If music is being played publicly at an event, the business needs to consider applicable public-performance licensing.

If a copyrighted song is being synchronized with video, the business may need synchronization permission.

If a specific sound recording is being incorporated into a production, master-use rights may also matter.

The question isn't simply, "Do we have music?"

The question is:

"Do we have the rights required for the way we're using that music?"

That's a completely different level of business thinking.

A DJ IS NOT YOUR COPYRIGHT DEPARTMENT.

Hiring a DJ, band, musician, production company, or entertainment provider does not automatically mean your company has obtained every copyright permission associated with the music being used.

The responsibility for licensing can depend on the specific use, venue, agreement, service, and rights involved.

Even BMI's licensing materials make the distinction between public-performance licensing and other rights such as synchronization, master use, and mechanical rights.

That means event strategy needs a rights-management component.

Before the event, somebody should be asking:

Who owns the music?

Who controls the rights?

What permission has been obtained?

What type of use does the permission cover?

Where can the music be used?

For how long?

Does the permission cover the live event?

Does it cover livestreaming?

Does it cover social media?

Does it cover advertising?

Does it cover video production?

Does it cover the website or application?

Those questions are not overkill.

They're professional risk management.

MUSIC LICENSING SHOULD BE BUILT INTO THE EVENT SYSTEM.

A professional event can incorporate a music-rights checklist alongside its other operational controls.

Identify the use. Is the music being performed live? Played by a DJ? Used as background music? Included in a livestream? Added to a promotional video? Used in social media? Embedded into an advertisement?

Identify the rights. Determine which rights may be implicated by the intended use.

Identify the rights holder or licensing organization. Different rights can be controlled by different parties.

Obtain the appropriate permission. Do not assume that one license covers every use.

Document the permission. Maintain agreements, licenses, invoices, terms, dates, territories, restrictions, and permitted uses.

Verify the actual use. A license should match what the company actually does with the music.

Maintain records. If someone asks years later how a piece of music was legally used, the answer shouldn't be: "I'm pretty sure we had permission."

Professional businesses keep receipts.

The U.S. Copyright Office provides copyright registration resources for musical compositions, sound recordings, motion pictures, websites, photographs, and other creative works.

For companies creating original content, understanding ownership and registration is another important part of intellectual-property management.

And for businesses licensing someone else's work, the central question remains the same:

What rights do we actually have?

That's professional event strategy.

It's not just making something impressive.

It's making something impressive and structurally sound.

I DON'T PLAN FOR PERFECTION. I PLAN FOR REALITY.

Events taught me another unavoidable lesson:

Something will go wrong.

People arrive late. People don't show. Equipment creates problems. Schedules move. Vendors need something. Communication gets missed. Weather changes. Staffing changes. Participants have questions.

That's reality.

The answer isn't pretending problems won't happen.

The answer is building enough structure to respond when they do.

Contingency planning means identifying what can go wrong, determining which problems actually matter, assigning responsibility, and creating alternatives before the pressure arrives.

That mindset has become part of how I approach business.

Don't just ask:

"What's the plan?"

Ask:

"What happens when the plan changes?"

That's where strategy gets real.

MARKETING MAKES THE PROMISE. OPERATIONS HAS TO KEEP IT.

Marketing can make something look incredible.

But the customer eventually experiences reality.

That's why marketing and operations cannot exist in separate worlds.

If the marketing promises professionalism, the operation needs to feel professional.

If the marketing promises an elite experience, the customer needs to experience one.

If the marketing promises convenience, registration can't feel like a scavenger hunt.

If the marketing promises community, the event needs to actually create connection.

Your marketing creates expectations. Your operations determine whether people trust you afterward.

That's why I don't see marketing as simply promotion.

I see it as part of the customer journey.

I LEARNED TO DESIGN THE EXPERIENCE FROM BOTH SIDES OF THE TABLE.

One of the biggest changes in my thinking came from realizing that there are two perspectives happening simultaneously.

There's the perspective of the organization producing the event.

And there's the perspective of the person experiencing it.

The organization thinks about:

Budget. Staff. Revenue. Equipment. Contracts. Sponsors. Schedules. Capacity. Risk.

The customer thinks:

Is this worth it? Do they know what they're doing? Is my child safe? Where do I go? What am I getting? Was this worth my money? Would I come back? Would I recommend this?

Great event strategy connects those two perspectives.

The organization needs its business objectives met.

The customer needs a reason to care.

When those objectives align, you have something powerful.

You have an experience that makes business sense and feels valuable to the audience.
