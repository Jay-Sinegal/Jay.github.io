How to Get Started as a College Student-Athlete: A Step-by-Step Scholarship and Recruiting Checklist

The ten-step operating system: pick your path, register with the NCAA Eligibility Center, build your academic and athletic file, build your college list, contact coaches, search for money separately, complete FAFSA and TOPS, and track everything.

By Jaylen Sinegal · September 24, 2026 · 11 min read

My first guide in this series answered the "understand the landscape" question: The Scholarship Is Not the Strategy. It explains what college athletics is, how the divisions work, where the money actually comes from, and how Louisiana athletes are already navigating it.

This guide is the operating system.

Here is exactly what you should do, where you should go, what you should collect, and when you should do it.

Not "student-athletes should understand the NCAA Eligibility Center." A real instruction: Step 1 is go create the account. The resource is at the end of the step. Follow the steps in order, and by the end you'll have a system instead of a dream.

Print this page. Your family is about to run a process.

STEP 1: DETERMINE WHICH COLLEGE-ATHLETICS PATH YOU ARE PURSUING

Before you do anything else, make one honest decision: which path are you actually pursuing?

  • NCAA Division I: largest schools, most competition, more athletic money (but still most of it partial). Uses the NCAA Eligibility Center, 16 core courses, 2.3 core GPA.
  • NCAA Division II: partial-scholarship model, more playing-time reality. Uses the NCAA Eligibility Center, 16 core courses, 2.2 core GPA.
  • NCAA Division III: no athletic scholarships; academics first. No Eligibility Center; you meet the school's own admission and financial-aid process.
  • JUCO / NJCAA junior college: a two-year on-ramp, often with athletic aid, then a transfer to a four-year school. No NCAA clearinghouse; meet NJCAA academic standards (roughly a 2.0 GPA in good standing).
  • NAIA: its own eligibility center and its own scholarship rules.

This decision changes everything downstream: which account you create, what you collect, and who you contact.

You don't need to commit to one school. You need to commit to a level of competition reality. You can pursue multiple levels at once, and that's smart. Just know that D1 and D2 both begin at the NCAA Eligibility Center, and everything else begins somewhere else.

STEP 2: CREATE YOUR NCAA ELIGIBILITY CENTER ACCOUNT

If you are targeting NCAA Division I or Division II: go create your NCAA Eligibility Center account now.

Start here: NCAA Eligibility Center (ncaa.org/eligibility-center). and register today, not the week a coach calls. Do not wait until a scholarship offer arrives to start this process. The Eligibility Center will not look at your file in a hurry because you're excited.

What you'll need:

  • Your legal name, birth date, email, and mailing address.
  • Your high school's information so your counselor can be linked to your account.
  • Your transcript and course information. Have them handy.
  • Payment ($110 for domestic academic and athletics certification as of September 2025) or a fee waiver if your family qualifies.

What your high school needs to provide:

  • Your approved core-course list.
  • Your official transcript at the end of the junior year and again after graduation, plus proof of graduation.

What you must monitor:

  • Your core-course GPA: not your transcript GPA. It's calculated only from NCAA-approved core courses on an unweighted 4.0 scale. Two different numbers, two different futures.
  • Your core-course completion status against the 16-course requirement, including the D1 "10/7" rule: 10 core credits, 7 in English, math, or science, completed before senior year.
  • Your eligibility status as it updates.

Verify with your counselor:

  • That every course you're taking is on the NCAA-approved list at your school.
  • That your schedule still hits the core-course distribution (English, math, science, social science, additional core).
  • When your initial transcript will be submitted: end of junior year is the standard; write it on the family calendar.

Mistakes to avoid:

  • Registering in the middle of senior year, when course-selection mistakes can't be fixed.
  • Comparing your 3.8 transcript GPA to the Eligibility Center's 2.3 threshold is comparing a weighted GPA to an unweighted core GPA.
  • Assuming your counselor knows you've registered. Tell them. They are your partner in this.

STEP 3: BUILD YOUR ACADEMIC FILE

Collect every academic document in one place now. You'll need copies for dozens of interactions: eligibility, financial aid, and every coach you will contact.

  • Current transcript: the official record a coach and the Eligibility Center both want to see.
  • Core-course information: the NCAA-approved version of your coursework, and your core-course GPA.
  • Your GPA: both the transcript GPA and the core-course GPA, clearly labeled so you never confuse them early in a conversation.
  • Graduation requirements: your school's requirements and your progress, because eligibility and graduation tracks must line up.
  • Test information where applicable: ACT/SAT scores. The NCAA no longer requires them for initial eligibility, but colleges and academic scholarships still use them.
  • Academic awards: honor roll, academic letters, National Honor Society, dual-enrollment credits.
  • Academic résumé: a one-page document that makes a coach's compliance review easy.

Why each one matters: a coach isn't just judging talent. They're judging risk. Can this athlete stay eligible, graduate, and not blow up our academic numbers? The easier you make that answer "yes," the more comfortable they are recruiting you.

STEP 4: BUILD YOUR ATHLETIC PROFILE

Now the part athletes actually enjoy, but build it like a professional, not like a fan page.

Build evidence.

  • Position/event: and secondary positions/events.
  • Height and weight where relevant.
  • Verified times or measurements: 40-yard dash, pro-agility, vertical, bench, or event-specific numbers from credible sources. The word "verified" is doing real work here.
  • Statistics: season by season, so a coach can project development.
  • Competition history: teams, tournaments, showcases, state and regional results.
  • Awards: all-district, all-state, records, honors.
  • Highlight film: short, current, labeled.
  • Full-game/event footage: the kill-your-highlight tape check for coaches who want truth.
  • References: coaches, trainers, counselors who will actually pick up the phone.
  • Contact information: the athlete's direct email and phone, not the family group chat.

A highlight reel is marketing. Verified performance data, competition history, academic information, and references give the coach something to evaluate. The reel gets attention; the file gets a response. Build both, and put the file first.

STEP 5: BUILD YOUR COLLEGE LIST

Do not wait for schools to find you first. Build your list of 20–25 schools yourself, and research them like you're buying into a business partnership.

Divide the 20–25 into categories based on your own research and judgment:

  • Schools the athlete is academically qualified for.
  • Schools where the athlete's athletic profile appears relevant.
  • Schools the family can realistically afford.
  • Schools the athlete would genuinely attend.
  • Schools with appropriate academic programs for their intended major.

I'm not giving you a "dream school / safety school" formula. The athlete makes those calls. Your job is to force the honest five-question pass over a real list, and to use the Louisiana landscape. Remember, every level of competition exists in this state, and the Louisiana college directory from the first guide is your starting map.

STEP 6: FIND THE COACHING STAFF

Here's the exact navigation and it works at almost every college in America:

College website → Athletics → Your Sport → Coaching Staff → recruiting / contact information

Record for each school on your list:

  • The head coach and the assistant coaches for your sport, with email addresses.
  • If there's a recruiting coordinator, a real person who manages prospects, their name and contact information.
  • The sport's "Prospective Student-Athlete" or recruiting questionnaire/link.

If the coaching staff link is missing, the sports-information office will answer. One email like "I'm a (class year) (position) prospect and I couldn't find a recruiting contact for (sport), can you point me to the right person?" gets you the answer every time.

STEP 7: CONTACT COACHES

"Contact coaches" is useless advice unless you know what the contact actually is. Every coach in America gets dozens of generic emails a week. This is where the athlete's brand gets built, or buried with the rest of the spam.

Send a short, complete, professional email. Include:

  • Athlete name and graduation year.
  • Position/event.
  • High school.
  • Academic information: GPA, core-course GPA, test scores if available.
  • Athletic measurements and verified numbers.
  • Relevant statistics.
  • Links to film (highlight and full game/event).
  • Upcoming competition: where a coach or scout can actually see them.
  • Contact information.
  • Why they're interested in that program: not "your school looks nice." Name something specific: the coach, a program success, academics, the fit.

Then link to the school's recruiting page so the coach has a formal path to respond. The message itself should be 100% about giving the coach easy ways to say yes, not about what you want the coach to do for you.

Your email is your first showcase. Send your best one to a practice school first, get feedback, then send the polished version to the schools that matter.

STEP 8: SEARCH FOR MONEY SEPARATELY

Do not run one search for "college scholarship" once and call the job finished. Run separate searches. Different money comes from different places.

  • Athletic scholarships: through each college's athletic department, per the division rules you now understand.
  • Academic scholarships: through each college's financial-aid office and admissions; merit money has its own criteria and deadlines.
  • Institutional scholarships: college-specific awards, often automatic for qualifying GPAs.
  • Need-based aid: the FAFSA, which drives Pell Grants, federal loans, and work-study at every college.
  • Louisiana scholarships: state-level awards beyond TOPS.
  • TOPS: apply via FAFSA or LOSFA by July 1 of the award year.
  • Private scholarships: foundations, corporations, and nonprofits; watch deadlines like a sport you love.
  • Local and community scholarships: civic clubs, churches, banks, and local businesses in your town. Less competition, same money.
  • Major-specific scholarships: for the degree track the athlete plans to pursue.

Official resources while you do this: the NCAA's scholarship and financial-aid page, LOSFA for everything Louisiana, and LHSAA for state high-school athletics programs. Never pay to apply for a scholarship.

STEP 9: COMPLETE FAFSA AND LOUISIANA FINANCIAL-AID PROCESSES

Do this like it's a game clock. It has real deadlines.

FAFSA:

  • Create your FSA ID at studentaid.gov. the parent creates theirs too.
  • Complete the FAFSA. The 2027-28 application opens to everyone by October 1, 2026. File early every year, not just senior year. FAFSA drives Pell Grants, federal aid, need-based aid at every college, and TOPS.
  • After filing, review your Student Aid Report and answer any verification requests from colleges immediately.

Louisiana (LOSFA):

  • Create a LOSFA (mylosfa.la.gov) account and use the Student Hub to track your awards.
  • Apply for TOPS through the FAFSA or a LOSFA application by July 1 of each award year. ACT and GPA thresholds decide the level: Opportunity (ACT 20, GPA 2.50), Performance (23, 3.25), Honors (27, 3.50), Excellence (31, 3.50), and TOPS-Tech for two-year programs (ACT 17, GPA 2.50).
  • Check the LA GO Grant (need-based) and the M.J. Foster Promise Fund if applicable. Both are administered through LOSFA.

This is where it all stacks: TOPS pays tuition, FAFSA opens need-based aid, college financial-aid offices add institutional money, and whatever athletic scholarship exists is layered on top. But only if you start the paperwork on time.

BEFORE YOU FINISH THIS GUIDE, YOU SHOULD HAVE

Ten steps, one verdict. If your family finishes this and can't check these boxes, the work isn't done:

  • NCAA Eligibility Center account
  • Current transcript
  • Core-course information
  • Athlete résumé
  • Highlight and full-game/event film
  • Verified athletic measurements
  • List of 15-25 potential colleges
  • Coach contact list
  • Scholarship tracker
  • FAFSA plan (2027-28 opens by October 1, 2026)
  • TOPS / LOSFA information (apply by July 1)
  • Application deadlines
  • Financial-aid deadlines

The scholarship is not the strategy. The checklist is.

Sources and references

The steps in this guide reflect the rules as I verified them in September 2026. These are my own summaries and checklists, not reproductions of copyrighted material. Verify everything against the official sources, because requirements change:

  • NCAA Eligibility Center: core-course and GPA requirements, initial eligibility, and the age-based rule transition.
  • NCAA Student-Athletes: Scholarships and Financial Aid: how athletic scholarships, equivalencies, and renewals work.
  • NJCAA (njcaa.org): junior college eligibility and athletic aid rules.

  • LOSFA (mylosfa.la.gov): Louisiana TOPS, LA GO Grant, and state financial-aid deadlines.
  • LHSAA (lhsaa.org): eligibility and sanctioning for Louisiana high school athletics.
