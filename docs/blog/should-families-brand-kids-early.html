Should Families Brand Their Kids Early? Hell Yes.

NIL isn't just for star athletes. Here's the research on goals, failure, and character, and why every kid can start building a brand before college.

By Jaylen Sinegal · September 4, 2026 · 12 min read

Let's get the question out of the way, because I know it's the one everybody's really asking: are we really about to talk about NIL for kids? Elementary school kids? The ones who still lose teeth and cry over broccoli?

Hell yes. And I'm going to tell you why that's not as wild as it sounds.

Failure Is Not an Option. It's a Fact.

Here's where we start, and I mean start — before goals, before branding, before a single dollar ever enters the conversation.

We all fail. Every single day. Missed shot, wrong answer, forgotten homework, bad note in the recital. That's not a hypothetical for your kid — it's Tuesday. So the question was never "how do we protect our kids from failure." That question doesn't exist. The real question is: how early do we teach them to build a healthy response to it?

Turns out — earlier than you think. A longitudinal study followed children from assessments at 14, 24, and 36 months through 5th grade; the researchers found that toddler emotion-regulation trajectories predicted later resilient functioning across physical, academic, cognitive, and social-emotional measures. This isn't only a teenage skill. Its foundation starts in toddlerhood. Read the longitudinal study.

And here's the part that should change how you praise your kid tonight: research distinguishes praise for process—effort, strategy, and persistence—from praise aimed at fixed personal traits. Longitudinal work has linked process praise received by toddlers with later incremental mindsets and academic achievement, while experimental research has found that intelligence praise can undermine motivation and performance. Review the American Psychological Association’s research summary.

We do not disguise structure with fun. We teach and grow the mindset that the journey itself is fun — failure included — so our kids learn to challenge challenges instead of avoiding them.

That's the foundation. Everything else in this article stands on it.

A Brand Isn't Vanity. It's a Documented Career Mechanism.

Once a kid has that foundation — the goals, the resilience, the "I haven't gotten there yet" mindset — something else becomes possible: they can start building an identity people recognize. And I need you to hear this part clearly, because "personal branding" gets treated like a buzzword, and it's not.

Peer-reviewed research involving 477 participants across two cultural contexts found that personal branding was positively associated with perceived employability and had a positive indirect relationship with career satisfaction through perceived employability. That does not prove the same outcomes for children, but it does show that personal branding can function as a documented career behavior in adults. Read the peer-reviewed study.

So when your kid starts figuring out who they are — the mathematician, the debate captain, the kid who's just relentlessly kind to everyone in the cafeteria — that's not "just a phase." That's brand-building. It was always going to happen. The only question is whether we help them do it on purpose.

NIL Was Never Supposed to Be Only for Athletes

Here's where it gets fun.

Everybody thinks NIL — Name, Image, and Likeness — is a sports thing. Nike signs a 13-year-old soccer prodigy, an 8-year-old football phenom carries a six-figure valuation, and the headline writes itself: "kid gets rich playing ball." Cool story. But it's the smallest version of what's actually happening in this country right now.

Let me show you the receipts.

Tani Adewumi

Chess

Won the New York State K–3 chess championship while his family lived in a homeless shelter. His story later led to publishing, film, Chess.com, and Target opportunities. Chess profile · Deal reporting

Salem Abdulrahman

Chess

Earned his FIDE master norm at 8. At 13, the UAE Chess Federation and Damas announced a Dh500,000 sponsorship supporting competitions, training camps, and a monthly stipend for three years. Contemporary report

Gitanjali Rao

Science

Developed technology addressing lead in water and later became TIME's first-ever Kid of the Year, a National Geographic Young Explorer, and a UNICEF Youth Advocate. USPTO profile · UNICEF announcement

Elisabeth Anisimow

Art

Developed an international audience as a young visual artist through her elaborate living-art compositions. Her example shows how distinctive work and a recognizable story can travel beyond a local audience.

Yoyoka Sano

Music

Started performing early and, by 8, had endorsement relationships with Pearl, Vic Firth, Zildjian, and other music brands. Official biography

None of these kids started with a deal. They started with a kid doing the thing — playing, researching, painting, drumming — long before anybody was paying them. The brand followed the character-building. Not the other way around. That's not a coincidence. That's the whole argument of this article, proven five different ways, in five different industries.

So no, your kid doesn't have to be the star athlete to get exposure, opportunity, or a check. They have to be themselves, consistently, long enough for the world to notice. Passing first grade is a goal. Practicing good hygiene is a goal. Landing a brand deal in your industry of choice is a goal built the exact same way — just with a bigger number attached.

The Substance Behind the Brand

I want to be straight with you about one thing: none of this works without the real skill underneath it. Chess, art, music, debate — these aren't just enrichment activities to keep a kid busy on a Tuesday afternoon. They can help develop the critical thinking, communication, and persistence that make a personal brand credible instead of hollow. Research has found positive associations between extracurricular participation and math achievement, grades, educational expectations, and later educational status, while also finding that excessive participation can reduce those benefits. Review the longitudinal findings.

A brand without substance is just noise. A brand with substance is a resume nobody else in the room has.

Louisiana Is Already Building the Door

If you're in Louisiana like me, here's your local receipts.

Louisiana Act 810, effective August 1, 2026, permits qualifying high-school interscholastic athletes to earn NIL compensation. Agreements involving athletes under 18 require written parental or guardian consent; the law restricts specified industries, requires disclosure of proposed agreements to the school, and allows schools to limit NIL activity during academic and official team activities. Read the enrolled act.

Louisiana Act 895, effective June 9, 2026, expands the state’s athlete-agent framework to representation involving student athletes, including high-school athletes. It requires registration, attorney-general-prescribed training, and a background check; specified violations of an agent’s statutory duties can carry felony penalties of $1,000 to $10,000 and up to five years of imprisonment. Read the enrolled act.

Worth knowing: these Louisiana provisions cover student-athletes; they do not create equivalent NIL rights for every talented student. Non-athletes can still build audiences and pursue ordinary creative or commercial opportunities under other applicable laws and school rules, but families should not assume the athlete-specific protections apply to them.

The Guardrails That Make This Safe to Chase

I know somebody's asking: is this actually ethical? Are we rushing our kids?

Here's my answer, and it's not a dodge: the guardrails already built into these laws — parental consent, agent background checks, banned industries, real criminal penalties for bad actors — aren't arguments against letting your kid chase this. They're the reason it's safe to let them chase it. A helmet doesn't argue against the bike ride. It's what makes the ride something you'd actually let your kid go on.

The one thing I will say clearly: build the mindset first. Goals. Resilience. Identity. Let the contracts and the deals follow whenever your kid — and the opportunity — are actually ready. Chasing the check before the character is built is the version of this that goes wrong. Building the character and letting the check find its way to a kid who's ready for it? That's just good parenting with better timing than any generation before us has ever had access to.

We Challenge Challenges

So here's where I land, and it's not complicated.

We do not disguise structure with fun. We teach the mindset that the journey itself is the fun — failure included, because failure is not an option, it's a fact, and we all live it every single day. What matters is what we build in response to it. And when a family does that early — really early, toddler-goals-and-first-grade-goals early — the kid doesn't just develop faster. They develop theirs. Their brand, their voice, their shot at opportunities most of us never had access to.

This isn't about turning your kid into a business. It's about giving them the tools to know exactly who they are before the world tries to tell them.
